# Direction: the one idea, the shape, the runtime

This file is about what the film is *about* and how it is built to move someone.
The rules that stop it looking generated are in the other references.

## One idea

Every film proves one sentence. Write it before anything else, in the plan's
`thesis`, and check every beat against it.

- Good: "Your page designs are files in your repo, beside your app."
- Good: "Understand code by watching it run in slow motion."
- Bad: "Caret is the future of design." (a feeling, not a claim)
- Bad: "See all the things our product can do." (a feature list wearing a sentence)

If a beat does not serve the thesis, it is a different film. Cut it.

## Structure

Both modes share a five-part spine. Walkthroughs and motion pieces differ in how
much of it is spent where, not in whether it exists.

1. **Hook** — the first 1–3 seconds. The payoff, the result, or the single most
   striking image. Not a logo, not a title card, not company history. Most visitors
   a demo can get leave during a 40-second warm-up.
2. **Setup** — the smallest amount of context that makes the payoff mean something.
   In a walkthrough this is usually zero: you are already inside the app.
3. **Proof** — the body. One workflow, or three beats of rhythm. This is the film.
4. **Turn** — the moment the thesis is undeniable: the file appears, the colour
   sweeps the page, the state flips, the third example lands.
5. **Close** — one caption, one action, one card. Name the next step. A demo that
   convinces and then stops is a leak.

**Open on the product, not on an introduction.** The first frame should be the app
working, or the strongest composed image the film has. The thesis can be the first
caption; it does not need a card.

For a walkthrough, the proof is causal: A happens, which makes B happen, which
produces C. If a beat does not cause the next, it is a detour.

For a motion piece, the proof is rhythmic: the same move, applied to three
different things, until the viewer can feel the system. The code-slowmo promo types
a question, the box becomes the answer, it folds back, and does it again twice.
That repetition *is* the argument.

## Runtime and format

Set both from where the film will be watched, then script to that length.

| Placement | Runtime | What it must do |
|---|---|---|
| Social feed | 15–30s | Land one feature or one moment |
| Homepage / README | 45–90s | The category and the core value |
| Product page | 90s–3 min | One full workflow |
| Onboarding | 2–4 min, or a series | Get a new user to first value |

Formats are built together from one script, never retrofitted:

- **9:16 vertical** — TikTok, Reels, Shorts. Keep the bottom 15% clear of anything
  the viewer must read; feed apps draw their buttons there.
- **4:5 phone** — X, LinkedIn, Instagram feed. Fills most of a phone screen.
- **16:9 wide** — YouTube, the README, desktop, a sales deck.
- **1:1 square** — some feeds, embeds.

Choose a primary format and say so; build the second cut from the same script. A
single 16:9 master pushed everywhere underperforms everywhere except the one place
it was made for. In portrait formats, desktop apps must be cropped to a readable
region: set a readability floor (how close the camera must be for the app's text to
read) and never carry text the viewer needs in a wider shot.

## Captions first, then motion

Write the captions as part of the plan, not after the render. They are the film's
argument for a muted viewer, which is most viewers. Rules and the banned list are in
`copy.md`; the shape is:

- one line per beat, under about nine words;
- it says what just happened, in the viewer's terms ("The edit is in the file.");
- it does not describe a UI control, because the UI is on screen;
- the product is the only brand it names.

## Honesty

The audience will try to reproduce what they saw. That is the standard.

- Every on-screen behaviour is something the real product does, in the order it
  does it, with its real interface. A scripted simulation may remove errors and
  waiting. It may never add capability.
- Compressing time is honest when it is visible: a cut, a speed ramp, or the app's
  own progress state. Hiding that time passed is not.
- Number claims only when measured. No fake testimonials, customer counts, logos or
  ratings.
- If a beat needs the product to do something it does not, cut the beat. The demo
  that outruns the product is the clearest tell of a product in trouble.
- Fictional demo content (a made-up customer, project or shop) is scenery: it must
  still be specific and believable, and the captions never name it.

## Intake

Find out, without asking a question you can answer yourself from the repo or the
web:

- what decision the film moves ("get a technical evaluator to start a trial");
- who watches it, and where (this sets length and format);
- what the product really does, and where the truth lives (source, screenshots, a
  running build);
- the brand: background, text and muted colours, accent, type family, logo rules;
- who owns approval, and whether they want to review the storyboard.

Then say the mode, the thesis, the runtime and the formats in one short reply, and
move to the storyboard.
